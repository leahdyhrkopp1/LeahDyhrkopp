Which AI Tool, When?

There are more AI tools than ever, and they don’t all do the same job. For my first Generative AI lab, I gave the same prompts to several of them and compared what came back, so I’d know which tool to reach for depending on the task and where each one can lead you astray.

Generative AI for Business (ITOM 6216)
Tools
ChatGPT · Gemini · Google AI Mode · NotebookLM

The assignment

The lab asked us to test AI search, chat assistants and research tools side by side on very different kinds of questions, from everyday advice to analyzing a document. I used my own undergraduate research paper for the document test, which made it easy to tell when a tool got something wrong.

What I tested
TaskWhat I askedTools compared
Everyday advice“Is it a good time to buy a house in Dallas?” and which master’s degree fits someone moving from political science into tech consulting or lobbying.Google Search, Google AI Mode and Gemini in the browser
Finding a sourceTrace a quote from a news story about a data breach back to the original article.ChatGPT, Gemini in the browser and Google AI Mode
Working with a documentSummarize my research paper, quote a passage with its page number and answer a question the paper doesn’t cover.ChatGPT, Gemini and NotebookLM
A personal question“What do you know about Leah Dyhrkopp?”ChatGPT, Gemini in the browser and Google AI Mode with personalization on
Pushing backChallenge each tool’s answer about me and see how it corrects itself.ChatGPT, Gemini in the browser and Google AI Mode
What I found
  • 01

    Everyday advice. All three tools gave sensible, sourced overviews of the Dallas housing market. Gemini in the browser read more like a conversation and ended with a clear verdict based on my situation.

  • 02

    Finding a source. ChatGPT traced the quote to the original AP article, with its date and link. AI Mode found the story through NBC and ABC, and Gemini in the browser pointed to a secondary site. Only one tool went straight to the original.

  • 03

    Working with a document. All three tools correctly said my paper doesn’t discuss AI in government programs instead of making something up. NotebookLM tied every point to a numbered citation from the paper, which made it the easiest to check.

  • 04

    A personal question. With memory or personalization on, the tools pulled real details about me but mixed in things that weren’t accurate. Without that context, Gemini in the browser found almost nothing reliable.

  • 05

    Pushing back. When I challenged their answers, the tools were quick to apologize and revise, sometimes too quick. ChatGPT even admitted its correction had introduced a new mistake by presenting an assumption as a verified fact.

My guide

Which tool I’d reach for.

Based on what I saw in this lab.

When I need…I’d use…
A quick overview of a broad questionGoogle’s AI Overview or AI Mode, then click through to the sources.
Advice for my specific situationA chat assistant like Gemini or ChatGPT, with enough context about what I’m deciding.
The original source of a claimChatGPT to find it, then I open the link and confirm it myself.
Answers about a specific documentNotebookLM, because every answer points back to the page it came from.
Anything about a real personAny tool as a starting point only, with every detail verified before I use it.
What I learned

The best AI tool depends on the job. Search-style tools are great for quick overviews, chat assistants are better for thinking through a decision and grounded tools like NotebookLM are the most trustworthy when the answer has to come from a specific document.

The biggest lesson came from asking about myself. Seeing confident answers that mixed true and false details about my own life showed me how easily AI can sound right without being right. Now I always check the source, and I push back when something seems off.
